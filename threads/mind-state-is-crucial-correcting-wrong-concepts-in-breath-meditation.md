# Mind State Is Crucial - Correcting Wrong Concepts in Breath Meditation

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **refugeinbuddha** · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · `Advice` · 10 points · 2015-12-17

<https://www.reddit.com/r/genuinemeditation/comments/3x5u3n/mind_state_is_crucial_correcting_wrong_concepts/>

Some people have asked why they cannot feel, see, hear, touch, etc their jhana-nimittas to a huge degree. They say that it is dull, it is soft, it is weak, on and on.

The jhana-nimitta is like the reflection in the mirror of your own mental state. It is like the shadow of your mind. It is deeply connected to the divine.

When you raise your hand, the reflection in the mirror raises its hand as well. In the same way, when you stabilize your mental state, the jhana-nimitta becomes still. When your mental state becomes powerful and absorbed, the jhana-nimitta becomes strong and powerful. Therefore, it is called a sign. It is produced as a consequence of deep absorption from your own mental state.

When your jhana-nimitta is dull, it means that your mind is dull. If it is enshrouded in darkness, it means that your mind is dark. If it is obscured by thoughts, it means that your mind is obscured by thought. If it disappears from fear, excitement or worry, it means that your mind has not built up equanimity. Perhaps it can be a reflection of your physical state from lethargy. If you are enlightened, even if you close your eyes, they will be filled with expansive light.

Perhaps it can be a lack of ethics in your life - Do not forget that the Buddha's path is the entire Noble Eightfold Path - not just meditation. The rest of the Eightfold path is interdependent.

Simply put, you should not strive to achieve the jhana-nimitta, but instead, to stabilize your own mental state. As a consequence of doing so, the reflection, the jhana-nimittas, that *indicate* your deep absorption, will arise.

How then, do you stabilize the mental state?

One thing I have realized through messages to me is that there is a huge misconception in the way people treat the instructions in the Anapanasati Sutta. For example, it is as quoted:

> **The Physical Body**

> Inhaling, exhaling, discerning that the breath is long/short.

> Inhaling, exhaling, sensitive to the physical body.

> Inhaling, exhaling, calming the physical body.

> **Sensations/Feelings**

> Inhaling, exhaling, sensitive to bliss (piiti) and joy (sukha).

> Inhaling, exhaling, sensitive to mental agitation/feelings.

> Inhaling, exhaling, calming the mental agitation.

> **Mind**

> Inhaling, exhaling, sensitive to the mind

> Inhaling, exhaling, satisfying the mind

> Inhaling, exhaling, steadying the mind

> Inhaling, exhaling, releasing the mind

> **Mental Qualities**

> Inhaling, exhaling, mindful of Inconstancy

> Inhaling, exhaling, mindful of Dispassion

> Inhaling, exhaling, mindful of Cessation

> Inhaling, exhaling, focusing on Relinquishment

The very first thing you must understand here, is that each of the categories are various ways of mindfulness. It also implies a progression.

The first 10 steps are literally the instructions of breath meditation leading into jhana. The last 4 of mental qualities are what we call "insight" meditation - This can be done either after the first 10 are done, or literally during any point, although you do not want to interrupt the process while getting into jhana.

Remember - the key is to understand what each of the steps are doing to your mind. It is not about the technique, but about what it does to the mind!

Yes, it is about training each step and moving on and on - But the process should become easier, not harder. You are not accumulating baggage along the way, but simplifying things and dropping things. It should become easier, not harder.

When you first have a meditation object, you perceive it through one of the 5 external senses. In this case, it is the tactile, because you perceive it through the physical body. You can even use another sense gate, as long as it continues to the next few steps and gets the mental state stiller than before.

Then, as you get more absorbed into your meditation object, the rest is let go of, including all the tension of your physical body. This is not about consciously forcing your body into relaxation. As a consequence of focusing on the breath, holding the right degree of "grip" on the object, like a delicate grasping of jelly such that it does not break, the body relaxes and calms down.

At this point, the breath becomes subtler and subtler, but it depends on whether you are doing it correctly. If you are doing this from nervous tension, it will restrict your level of joy and bliss.

You should be feeling the breath as a whole - not tying it to any particularly specific location such as the nose. As I explained in a previous post, anapana is in fact about the expansion and contraction of energy, not about tying your sensation at the nose. In fact, this restricts the entire process and makes it harder to experience full body bliss and joy. If you are a beginner trying to train it, you can certainly use the nose, but once you get the hang of it, go for the entire breath.

You don't have to strain at all! It is all about simply "knowing". You don't need to concentrate to know that the breath is there. You must simply "tie" your attention in a way such that you are mindful when the breath is coming in, for its entire length, and when the breath is going out, for its entire length.

Another point to note is that you should never be controlling your breath. You may certainly do deep breathing to calm the breath down at the start. However, you must let go and become mindful. You must get rid of all forms of "doing" and "grasping" and trying to control things. You will never control your way into a jhana - in a jhana, while there is slight wavering in first jhana, a lot of "doing" has disappeared.

I want you to think of it as an onion. At the very start, you have a huge piece of onion. That is the very first meditation object that you have. You then peel away the skin of the onion one layer by one layer. The meditation object becomes subtler and subtler.

Therefore, you should not be adding more and more - your meditation object should go from coarse to subtle to even more subtle levels. What am I talking about here?

It is moving from the physical senses - to the sensations - to the mind! That is what they mean by "withdrawal of senses".

Then we get to the sensations reference point. At this point, if your physical body has calmed sufficiently, and your attention has been placed in a good degree of "grip", such that you do not have muscular tension, and yet have a good enough awareness to not be distracted away from the meditation object, then you can introduce the sensitivity to bliss and joy.

Bliss and joy is not something you can think your way to. You must feel it. Where do you feel it? Joy radiates from the heart. When you communicate with someone, you speak "heart to heart".

In the process of watching your breath, you must open your heart and feel this beautiful, subtle, underlying joy and bliss. At first it can be subtle, as if you were having a mini-vacation. Then it glows like ember, becoming stronger and stronger.

The Buddha described it as gathering bath powder with water. It is as if you are packing as much joy and bliss as possible within your experience, such that your entire body is filled with it. However - Remember that you should not be consciously spreading it. You should simply just let go of any doing, and just take note of the bliss and joy. The more your mental state stabilizes, the more the bliss and joy grows.

Therefore, the key point here is that your watching of the breath should never be a dreadful watching! It should never be something that has no blissfulness behind it. It is the blissfulness that indicates your mental state becoming "pliant" and flexible.

What then, is "mental agitation"?

You will notice thoughts jumping out, trying to disturb the process. They can give rise to all kinds of things, such as fear, excitement, worry, etc.

This can happen at any point. You may even see the "weaker" jhana-nimittas appear - tingling, high pitched sounds, bright lights, flashing lights, floating orbs, etc. Then your mind, being unused to this, starts to have thoughts of excitement. For example, you might be new to the feeling of bliss and start to feel excited or fearful. Destabilizing the mind from its object, the jhana-nimitta suddenly disappears. This is especially so for the luminous jhana-nimitta.

Therefore, one has to have "tranquility" - which is the quality of a still mind that does not arouse in any way in response, allowing the absorption to continue as it is.

From here on, it is all about peeling the onion and getting to subtler and subtler qualities of the meditation object. For the breath for example, you will find that the subtle quality of the breath is the joy and bliss behind it. This is difficult to describe and you should practice as such.

If you cannot feel joy or bliss, then your heart is blocked - you are subconsciously suppressing it. The remedy for this, is to invoke the feeling of love in any way. Start with people that you love, or things that you enjoy, or simply relaxing and imagining becoming as comfortable as possible. Then train your lovingness to extend to other things.

Use the previous thread, "The Power of the Heart", to allow lovingness on your own qualities. This is like training wheels. Imagine a beautiful light shining from your heart and extending outwards. Get out of your head, and feel with your heart.

Hope this helps, and strive well for enlightenment.
