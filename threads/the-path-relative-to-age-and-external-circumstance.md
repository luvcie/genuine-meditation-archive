# The path relative to age and external circumstance.

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **blasenanda** · *(OCR)* · `Question` · 6 points · 2 months ago

Hello everyone. Iam 21 years of age and in university. My path started when I was 17 after an experience quite similar to the dark night of the soul, afterwards I began to read extensively on the experience, acquiring a lot of knowledge which over time drove me towards eastern philosophies which then led to meditation. For all this time I have felt drawn to the desire for liberation from human suffering and enlightenment. My concern though is, at my age I feel an immense pressure to live "normally” i.e. get a job, some money, a good family...I feel as though this is a hindrance and has often made me feel hopeless about my circumstances. To be honest it's not that part of me doesn’t want to be materially successful as I feel it will create a favourable condition for seeking enlightenment. But I wonder if I should abandon the path for a later age or keep going. Not forgetting the intensity of the sense pleasures in a person my age which is a constant battle. On more information on my practice, I haven't yet reached jhana but I am striving forward.I one day hope to achieve enlightenment and thus elevate the collective consciousness. I am quite glad and thankful to have found this virtual sangha.

  > I feel an immense pressure to live "normally” i.e. get a job, some money, a good family...I feel as though this is a hindrance and has often made me feel hopeless about my circumstances.

  Where does this pressure stem from? Is it external pressure from your parents, societal pressures and expectations, or is it something you wish to fulfill internally?

  I would hazard to say it is the former - external expectations and pressures.

  We are all conditioned from young to believe that such things as an education, a job/career, house, partner, children, and family will bring us happiness and 'success'.

  The truth of the matter is that for most people it will not bring them happiness, which is why so many have mid-life crises and feel lost, or fall into vices of alcohol, drugs, adultery, video games, TV shows etc. They are all means for them to escape from their current state of suffering.

  Also parents themselves get caught up in this trap and due to egos, they want their children to follow the same path as it is a reflection on them. If they're children are 'successful' and 'happy’, then they are seen as 'successful’ parents - even though what they are pressuring their children to do actually causes unnecessary suffering and unhappiness for their children, the exact opposite of what they truly want.

  Happiness is never guaranteed in life. Suffering and unfactoriness is however always guaranteed. So what type of suffering would you like to experience? The suffering attached to the mundane world for countless aeons? Or the suffering from letting go and sacrificing the mundane world and activities to reach liberation and awakening?

  Because enlightenment is the only true path to the cessation of suffering i.e. contentment.

  The #1 regret most people have on their deathbeds is that they did not live a life true to themselves, they instead lived a life that others expected of them. (Look up Bronnie Ware, top 5 regrets of the dying)

  Would you like to join them?

  You already know the answer, most of the time those things you mentioned are a hindrance (for the most part, they may also be highly beneficial for others). A job, some money, and especially a family with children all take up your energy, time, and effort, which could be directed towards cultivating.

  However do not be mistaken that you cannot remain on the path while you have/experience/do worldly things. The two are not separate or mutually exclusive to one another. They are one in the same.

  I fell under this trap and limiting belief for quite some time until Paramananda opened my eyes. Whatever you do, wherever you go, whoever you see, there in lies the Dhamma and the Path. You can cultivate no matter what it is you

  do.

  Be mindful. Know.

  Think kindly, act kindly, speak kindly, be loving. The Noble Eightfold Path must emanate through everything that you do and in this way you will always be cultivating and walking the path.

  > To be honest it's not that part of me doesn't want to be materially successful as I feel it will create a favourable condition for seeking enlightenment. But I wonder if I should abandon the path for a later age or keep going.

  Do not be fooled that the two are separate paths, they are the same! Yes one requires material finances to be able to live within the world and survive, however in reality one does not require much if one lives on the essentials and a simple life. Do not spend extravagantly, live a simple minimalist life and let go of all of the baggage and material goods that cause you suffering.

  One does not need a big house, nor a fancy car, or the latest smartphone to survive and pursue cultivation.

  Do not abandon the path for a later age. Instead, ask yourself honestly "What would you have to sacrifice to have what you want?" make a list and answer this question honestly.

  Now ask yourself "What are you willing to sacrifice?

  If they do not match up, then either you work towards sacrificing more of what you must, in order to get what you want, otherwise you must honestly and truthfully answer what it is that you actually want and you live up to that.

  If you are not willing to let go and sacrifice, then you clearly do not want it bad enough - for now at least. Perhaps you will mature and suffer more as you age, or you perform great deeds and accumulate merit to the point where you begin to cultivate sincerely and earnestly.

  > Not forgetting the intensity of the sense pleasures in a person my age which is a constant battle.

  One of the big distinctions however is that life is not black or white. The Buddha himself preached The Middle Way. You do not have to sacrifice all of these things at once nor imo would it be skilful whatsoever because it only sets one up for failure.

  For anything worth doing takes time and it takes effort, diligence, persistence and repetitive intentional actions to attain what one wants.

  No one goes to the gym on their first day being able to squat 300 kgs or bench press 150 kgs. No, they go day after day, week after week, month after month, and year after year until they slowly build up to that weight.

  As is the same when it comes to the things you must let go of and sacrifice. You do it piece by piece, step by step, brick by brick, and habit by habit. Work on the smaller things first (such as earnestly keeping the 5 precepts, but you first start with only one of them as your primary focus until it becomes an unconscious habit) and then work your way

  up.

  I am 24 myself and understand how you feel and where you are coming from. The big change in perspective (or from incorrect view to right view) came when I realized if I was serious about this, I must work diligently towards centering my life around Nibbana.

  I must lead a life directed at Nibbana and not fool, trick, or deceive myself into believing it was something I wanted if I was trying to do other things leading me away from it.

  So you must view everything you do in life (not literally everything, but work up from the minor aspects into the major ones) and question whether (1) Does doing this lead one to Nibbana? (2) If not, how may I change it so that it does? Must I work towards cutting it off or can I adjust the trajectory of it so that it becomes skilful?

  I am no where near where I want to be but with each passing day I am getting closer and closer. With my worldly work, I see it as a means to attain enough financial resources such that I may change my schedule so that instead of working many hours a week and meditating 1-1.5 hours each day, I will be able to do the opposite (or something closer to it as I work on skewing the ratio of time spent in favor of meditation/cultivation.)

  I have also chosen an occupation where I am able to gain merit for provision of my services (or at least not accumulate negative karma).

  You must frame and see everything you do in terms of the path, is it helping you along the path, or is it taking you away from it? Remember: worldly activities and the path are not separate, they are one in the same.

  When you make your sole purpose Nibbana you will see how this can be the case.

  > Who strives not when he should strive, who, though young and strong, is given to idleness, who is loose in his purpose and thoughts, and who is lazy - that idler never finds the way to wisdom. -Buddha

- **refugeinbuddha** · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · 4 points · 2 months ago

  > But I wonder if I should abandon the path for a later age or keep going.

  First, people say: "I need a career first."

  Then, people say: "I need a spouse first."

  Then, people say: "I need to take care of my Kids first."

  Then, people say: "I need to retire first."”

  Then, people say: "I need to travel the world and enjoy myself first."

  Then, people say: "I need to take care of my grandkids first."

  Then, people say: "I need to stay healthy first."

  Even on the deathbed, people say: "I'm too unhealthy to cultivate!”

  The problems in life are incessant. As the Lord Buddha declared:

  > “When an uninstructed worldling, O monks, comes upon gain, he does not reflect on it thus: ‘This gain that has come to me is impermanent, bound up with suffering, subject to change.” He does not know it as it really is. And when he comes upon loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, he does not reflect on them thus: ‘All these are impermanent, bound up with suffering, subject to change.” He does not know them as they really are. With such a person, gain and loss ... pleasure and pain keep his mind engrossed. When gain comes he is elated and when he meets with loss he is dejected. When fame comes he is elated and when he meets with disrepute he is dejected. When praise comes he is elated and when he meets with blame he is dejected. When he experiences pleasure he is elated and when he experiences pain he is dejected. Being thus involved in likes and dislikes, he will not be freed from birth, ageing and death, from sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, and despair; he will not be freed from suffering, I declare. “But, O monks, when an instructed noble disciple comes upon gain, he reflects on it thus: ‘This gain that has come to me is impermanent, bound up with suffering, subject to change.” And so he will reflect when loss and so forth come upon him. He understands all these things as they really are, and they do not engross his mind. Thus he will not be elated by gain or dejected by loss; elated by fame or dejected by disrepute; elated by praise or dejected by blame; elated by pleasure or dejected by pain. Having thus given up likes and dislikes, he will be freed from birth, ageing and death, from sorrow, lamentation, from pain, grief, and despair; he will be freed from suffering, I declare.

  There are only two mistakes on the path: Not starting, and stopping.
