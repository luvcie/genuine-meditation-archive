# On Doubting a Teacher

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **He_of_the_Hairy_Arms** · *(OCR)* · `Discussion` · 3 points · 8 months ago

Hello, sangha,

Today I visited a local Theravedan monk for the third time, and walked away with serious doubts about him as a potential teacher. I visited him because I wanted someone to help guide my meditation and spiritual development in person, in addition to Paramananda's advice and that of others here. I was hoping for someone whose guidance would be compatible with what I've discovered from experience and what my best reasoning tells me is the correct understanding of the teachings. Unfortunately, what I got was something very different, and I'm having trouble deciding whether I'm being arrogant or judicious in my doubt.

The first thing I noticed about this teacher was his long-windedness. He talks a very long time, sometimes meandering through several subjects until I'm not sure if he remembers the question. Sometimes I'm reluctant to ask for clarification because I don't want to frustrate him or seem recalcitrant. The second red flag (to me) was that he interprets "piti" to mean "peace of mind"” instead of "bliss," and asserts that extremely blissful states shouldn't come from simple attention to the sensations of breathing. His interpretation of the "bliss" of jhana is a very mildly pleasant peacefulness. This is inconsistent with experiences I've had, and with the teachings of several teachers I trust, including Paramananda, Ajahn Brahm, and Leigh Brasington, among others. This isn't to say that every teacher who references blissful states is without flaws; just that there are several prominent teachers of the jhanas who use the term "piti" to mean precisely "bliss," and that this aligns better with my experience. I also mentioned to him the sutta that describes "piti" as the feeling a desert wanderer has when he sees water and shelter on the horizon, and "sukha" as the feeling he has when he drinks the water and basks in the shade. This teacher suggested that that sutta was a good example of the difference between piti and sukha, but a bad indicator of their intensity. In my experience, the intensity of piti was exactly like that of the wanderer who sees water and shelter, with no exaggeration whatsoever.

There are also other disagreements. He claims that in his experience, a strong feeling of bliss from meditation is the result of something happening outside of the meditation itself. He described the time that his teaching career was going particularly well and how this, in his opinion, resulted in the one experience of meditative bliss that he's had. But this again simply does not square with my own experience. The first time I experienced jhanas, I was very solitary, lonely, and stressed out by graduate school. It also contradicts the story of the Buddha's discovery of the eightfold path in the suttas, in which he realizes that he "should not be afraid of the bliss derived from withdrawal from unwholesome states." It seems to me that this particular teacher harbors that exact fear.

How to handle this? Again, I'm having a hard time believing that I, the novice, could be the one who is right about these issues. This man is a monk with many more years' practice than I have. And it's the very nature of ignorance to think one knows better than the teacher who has been doing it for decades. Yet my experience and reasoning give me a clear answer.

Curious to hear the sangha's thoughts.

  Piiti (Bliss) belongs to mental formations (sankhara).

  Sukha (Joy) belongs to feelings (vedana). This comes from the interactions of the four elements of the body.

  If one understands Buddhist theory, there are three feelings: Pleasant, unpleasant and neutral. In the 4th jhana, there is freedom from joy (asukha) and suffering (adukkha). This means that Sukha is eventually seen as coarse and is dropped by the practitioner in the 4th jhana.

  One must realize that one does not leave the four foundations of mindfulness throughout Anapanasati, but instead one is going through a systematic process of seeing all of them.

  One must realize that to truly enter genuine jhana, one has to transform the body into a completely healthy one, such that bodily agitations do not pose a problem. This also means that one has to transform the chi channels, and fill the channels with inner-chi. This means that all the channels are open, including the front, back, left, right and center channels. All the tiny nadis throughout the body are also open. Until then, one is only receiving just a tiny bit of bliss. Even if you receive bliss, you are still grasping onto form - when you are reborn, you enter the Form realms and have not transcended samsara.

  What is the use of grasping onto this bliss? Is it of any use? The more you grasp, the more it escapes!

  Understand that everything has an empty nature! The moment you try to grasp it, it is already gone. How can you find it at all!

  Why are you taking them so seriously? They are all ‘common practices' - They are found in Christianity, Taoism, Hinduism, etc. What is so special about them? We cannot deny that it is an achievement, but it still is a long way towards the genuine Dharma.

  What is 'uncommon' and different in Buddhism is one's view, one's wisdom. If you simply sit here, and observe in your mind, investigating all the various functions of your mind - What is permanent? What is impermanent? Why does the background thought not empty out? Why is my body still felt? Scientific questions, scientific investigation - this is what you should have.

  A teacher points the way with a finger, don't look at the finger! Even if you look at the moon he is pointing, you can't reach it! Look inside yourself!

          A teacher does not have spiritual authority over you. In the end, there is no entity of a 'teacher’ or a 'student'. They are all labels, concepts, just part of a Universal Mind. Anyone who sticks to labels does not clearly recognize that if your perception is pure, everywhere are the Bodhisattvas and Buddhas.

          Therefore, one should study well in the principles of the Dharma. Study the sutras and master the principles. One should be able to answer any question thrown to you and not be confounded.

          Buddhism is a science of life and death. It is step-by-step, it is from observation, it is from experimenting. If science proves things otherwise, things have to change to fit what is real. The ultimate teacher is reality.

          However, a wise teacher is very valuable to prevent a student from going into pitfalls in subtler principles - this is not wrong. So yes, a good teacher is very valuable. But until you do meet one, it is important to have a mind of investigation, which is one of the factors that culminates in enlightenment. If you read the suttas, the Buddha always describes things very methodically and systematically - "If this happens, I try this. If that happens, I try this. Eventually, it is concluded that this and that is wrong, and that X is the answer."”

          If your principles are strong, you would not need to fear listening to a 'bad' teacher, because you will immediately recognize what is real and what is not. Therefore, a good study of the sutras - both Mahayana and Hinayana - is important.
