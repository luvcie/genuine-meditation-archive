# Absorption, not concentration

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **refugeinbuddha** · *(OCR)* · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · `Advice` · 7 points · 10 months ago

This is a crucial difference that I wish to clarify with my dear friends.

When one concentrates, it is the principle of starvation. By concentrating attention towards one single object, one strains energy. This energy is used to repress all other thoughts, and this is just like holding one's breath. Eventually, when one releases the effort, the hindrances arise again.

There is no Four Noble Truths in this, this is not what the Buddha taught. In fact, this is what the Buddha rejected when he practiced the Samadhi of No-Thought. “No Thought" itself was a big thought!

The Four Noble Truths was one of the first few sermons that the Buddha gave, and this is for a very good reason.

Firstly, you must recognize suffering.

What is suffering? If you wish to truly understand suffering, you must be able to observe interdependent origination in its working. You can watch how a thought arises, followed by a craving associated with it. You can watch how hindrances arise in you. You can watch how bodily or mental obstructions arise in you.

It is not about pressing them down. It is never the issue of 'force’.

Instead, it is the principle of non-dwelling. How does one non-dwell? One non-dwells in body, speech and mind.

If you cannot understand this, then at least non-dwell in body. What does this mean? When hindrances arise in you, you must see exactly how it has risen.

Why has it risen? How has it risen? What is the craving and response that your mind has given to this hindrance?

Seeing this, one sees the condition of suffering. This is the first Noble Truth.

When you feel bodily agitation, what should one do? One must recognize that this bodily agitation is also associated with a mental agitation, and release both. When a mental agitation has risen, the bodily agitation too, must also be released.

This is extremely clear as laid out by the Buddha in the Maha Saccaka Sutta:

> - B There are, Master Gotama, some brahmans & contemplatives who live committed to the development of the body but not to the development of the mind. They are touched by bodily painful feeling. It has happened in the past that when one (of them) was touched by bodily painful feeling, his thighs would grow rigid, his heart would burst, hot blood would gush from his mouth, he would go mad, out of his mind. His mind was thus subservient to his body and fell under the power of the body. Why was that? A lack of development of the mind. ' e o o o o o o o o o e S R S R R S S B G S B S R R B G S R S S S T S S S B S S S S R B S R R G B S S S R G R S R S e R R D S R S B R SR B R G R S R R R - A

As well as:

> "Then there are some brahmans & contemplatives who live committed to the development of the mind but not to the development of the body. They are touched by mental painful feeling. It has happened in the past that when one (of them) was touched by mental painful feeling, his thighs would grow rigid, his heart would burst, hot blood would gush from his mouth, he would go mad, out of his mind.His body was thus subservient to his mind and ' fell under the power of the mind. Why was that? A lack of development of the body. The thought has occurred to me that the disciples of Gotama the contemplative live committed to the development of the mind but not to the development of the body."

Therefore, if you only release the thought, but do not tranquilize the body, as expressed in the Buddha in his Anapanasati instructions, then you are only releasing it partially. This stress of the body is not just below the neck - A lot of stress can be in the head, wrapped around the entire brain! This is why people can get headaches. Real change can only come when you recognize it fully and release it completely.

Why do we have hindrances? Simply because we have violated precepts. In fact, they are not precepts, but things one does for karma not to lead to these unwholesome states. For countless, innumerable lifetimes, one has plagued oneself with breaking of these precepts. Therefore, one stops in body, speech and mind - and it is only in these that one frees oneself with the Noble Eightfold Path.

One should thus be skilfully mindful.

Why did the Buddha say "skilfully"? Simply because it is a skill - Recognition of the state is a skill.

Secondly, it is also a skill for one to train - to tranquilize the bodily agitation as well as the mental agitation.

This is all part of Right Effort - where one abandons unwholesome states rightly and acts towards wholesome states.

Eventually, one is effortlessly absorbed and the individual frustrations and agitations start to arise clearly in front of you, just like ripples in a massive pond.

Please see this and correct your meditation. This is directed to a friend in the sangha, but is also very useful for everyone else.

This is a huge problem in the Buddhism community, for many are cultivating 'concentration practice' which suppresses hindrances temporarily but does not offer permanent re-training - This is Brahman meditation, one can certainly develop special siddhis, but this is not the ultimate liberation that the Buddha has painstakingly taught.

When in doubt, one should always refer to the words of the Buddha, and test it in your own experience and observation.

            - **refugeinbuddha** · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · 3 points · 10 months ago

              The first jhana is not to be experienced, but to be practiced as part of the Noble Eightfold Path, transitioning into the fourth Jhana, later on into the ayatanas of infinite space, consciousness, and beyond. The first four jhanas, however, are the prerequisite for one who has entered the stream, aka the authentic Noble Eightfold Path.

              If one experiences first jhana, but does not sustain it, then this is not genuine dhyana. It is akin to the flicker of a lighter which gives a glimpse, but does not muster enough condition to start a fire. What is the reason for this? It is the lack of skillful everyday mindfulness, which is a reason why the Buddha advocated one to leave to practice in seclusion.

              I read your replies, and it is true that jhana is characterised by ecstasy, and that there is a huge time dilation - since time and space are characteristics of a discriminative mind. However, in the face of authentic jhana, these are degrees of non-dualities, transcending the previous. As such, it is also required that one sees 'joy' as coarse - and transcend it into higher states of subtler bliss, so that one makes it to the second jhana and beyond.

              Jhana is not something you attain for a while - it is a state one reaches and sustains, whereby one's entire habitual energies have been burned away.

      I like this teaching by Shinzen Young: Suffering = Discomfort x Resistance

      Sounds like you're struggling with maintaining equanimity with what you are experiencing. One strategy to develop equanimity is to 'divide and conquer’ so to speak. Discriminate between these different sensory experiences:

      e the physical-type body sensation you've described as 'pain’ e the mental talk happening e any mental imagery happening e the emotional-type body sensation that might be arising (sounds like it could be flavours of anger)

      Discriminate each of those sensory experience - when they 'coagulate' and merge with each other, the subjective experience becomes overwhelming.

      Shinzen puts it like this: If you have 10 units of negative mind chatter, 10 units negative mind imagery, 10 units negative physical body sensations - you have 10 + 10 + 10 = 30 units of discomfort in actuality. However when you allow them coagulate together because of unawareness, they multiply. So it becomes 10 x 10 x 10 = 1000 units of discomfort instead. That's exponentially more discomfort - 33x more with this loose example.

      So if you can discriminate between what you are experiencing from moment to moment without letting each one merge and multiple with each other then you'll be able to 'divide and conquer' the pain so to speak. You can divide much more than just what I've said above. They go into sub-components and sub-subcomponents too for each category.

      Last thing I'll add is a nice definition of equanimity:

      Learning to love every sensory experience as it arises but not to hold onto it inappropriately as it passes.
