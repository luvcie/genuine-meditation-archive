# Answer to "How do I even get stillness of mind?"

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **refugeinbuddha** · *(OCR)* · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · `Question` · 9 points · 1 year ago

Ju/masqwerty12345 messaged me and this seems to be a helpful question for many, therefore I have decided to post the answer here.

> I have also heard this. What if I have the wrong technique? I need to achieve stillness of mind in order to get to jhana, right? I'm not sure how to do that. Absolutus says that one can get to jhana within a quick time span if he/she has the correct technique. Quality over quantity. Does you meditation technique include this? Simply being aware of the breath? How do I even get stillness of mind?

The body and mind are linked. Therefore, both body and mind have to be tranquilized together. How to do this? Firstly, place your attention on the breath, so that it does not jump around to thoughts that surface. Secondly, use the breath to relax the body. Do not consciously control the breath, let the body do whatever it wants - you are simply observing.

When this happens, the first thing is that your body will become extremely relaxed and your breath will naturally slow down. As long as you do not shift your mind away from the breath (your meditation object), the attention will slowly become one-pointed, such that you notice nothing else but the breath. The physical sensations ebb away, the thoughts do not surface as much. Then as you let go more and more, paying attention to the breath, you find that the breath slowly turns into a joyful breath. It may become light and faint, but if you hold your attention properly, you might find that your body starts to feel tingling pleasure in various areas, such as your fingers and toes. It can vary for every session.

Maintain the mental state and keep letting go of any "doing". You do not "do" anything, but just watch. Just pay attention to the breath and it will slowly turn from a physical experience of the breath into a mental experience where the breath feels "beautiful” and joyful. It will start to become effortless.

If you find your mind becoming sluggish and distracted, then you must balance your attention to the bliss properly. If you strain too hard, you tense up the physical body and agitate the mind. If you loosen too much, the mind and body lose energy and you fall into sluggishness and dullness. Imagine riding a horse, or grasping a jelly in your hand. You want the right pressure, but yet you are not doing anything, simply going with the flow.

Any kind of technique brings you from the 6 senses (sight, hearing, touch, smell, taste, thought) to a pure mental object that normally manifests as what I call the signs of deep absorption (jhana-nimitta). Jhana-nimitta is also possibly what one might know as a "pure object” within the citta of the mind. Some yogi call it the presence of the Divine.

The most common jhana-nimitta is what people experience in the tactile sense as body tingling, which then progresses on a subtler form of tingling as absorption goes deeper.

The second most common jhana-nimitta is what some might call flashing lights, in the visual sense. Auras, bright lights, stars, orbs, strobing light, fields of light, colors, etc fall under this category.

The third most common jhana-nimitta is the auditory sense, where a high-pitched ringing can be perceived. It can be roaring, bells, whistling, bird-noises, crickets, etc. The main characteristic of this is that it tends to radiate from the center of the experience and is not tilted to any side, which differentiates itself from medical tinnitus.

The fourth is the kinesthetic sense, where one might start to feel spinning, becoming larger/smaller, movement, etc.

The fifth is the taste sense, where one might taste sweet saliva, nectar, etc.

The sixth is the olfactory sense, where one might smell roses, different types of flowers or even incense.

The list goes on, but the main thing is that all of these senses tend to slowly converge as absorption increases - which is akin to that of seen in synesthesia. All of them culminate into a single sensory field.

All of them do not matter, the only thing you should know is that when the original meditation object (breath, body, etc) disappears from perception, you should pay attention to the jhana-nimittas that are a mental representation of your state of mind.

The only way deeper is to find grades of clinging and to let go of them, which will bring you deeper than before.

What brings you out of these absorption states is your mental state being overexcited (fear/excitement) or losing energy (sluggish/torpor). Equanimity is what will guide you onwards.

The last thing to note is this - What is the point of attaining deep samadhi states? When you get off the mat and stop practising, the samadhi state fades away. Therefore, it becomes unreliable and is not the best practice one can have.

True practice is when one brings whatever they have acquired on the mat to the real world, in other words, one guards their mind from the six sense doors and continuously turns towards enlightenment.

By gaining right samadhi (as one of the folds of the Noble Eightfold Path), one then can use this "mental pliancy" to penetrate into the truths of things around them. One will gain 'insight' into the nature of suffering and the four noble truths. That is what one must do.

Jhana and Insight come hand-in-hand.

Strive well and may you attain awakening in this lifetime.
