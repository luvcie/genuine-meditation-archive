# Hard vs soft jhana?

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **philosophyguru** · *(OCR)* · `Question` · 9 points · 2 years ago

After watching some videos from Kenneth Folk on jhana, I realized that I may have incorrect assumptions about jhana because my mental image of jhana is based on descriptions that lie towards the hard end of the spectrum (e.g. if you are in fourth jhana, you are so withdrawn from sensory experience that you wouldn't notice if a bomb went off in your lap).

So, I have several questions.

1) For the purposes of investigating the three characteristics, is there a benefit to attaining hard jhanic states rather than soft jhana?

2) When jhana is described as an unmistakably different quality of consciousness, is that only true of hard jhana or also soft jhanic experience?

3) If one attains merely soft jhana, how can one stabilize the experience so that the mind does not lose concentration?

- **refugeinbuddha** · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · 3 points · 2 years ago

  Always go back to the suttas, not commentaries or a meditation teacher when you want to know the real truth. You will never know if a meditation teacher has really attained a fruit or have simply repeated information that they have learnt from a teacher, who may also be dissipating misinterpreted teachings. Even from me. Even if I verify what my teachers say. What you should do, is to investigate it through your own experience. If you look at the original Pali scriptures, the jhanas are characterized by the following.

  e First Jhana: vitakka (applied attention), vicara (sustained attention), ekaggatha (one-pointedness), sukha (happiness/joy), piti (blissfulness).

  e Second Jhana: Born of tranquility - unification of awareness free from vitakka/vicara, ekaggatha (one-pointedness), sukha (happiness/joy), piti (blissfulness), passaddhi (tranquility).

  e Third Jhana: ekaggatha (one-pointedness), sukha (happiness/joy), piti (blissfulness), passaddhi (tranquility) and upekkha (equanimity).

  e Fourth Jhana: Abandonment of pleasure (sukha) and pain (dukkha), ekaggatha (one-pointedness), piti (blissfulness), passaddhi (tranquility), upekkha (equanimity). Pure bright awareness.

  There is no such thing as a "hard" or "soft" jhana. Jhana is jhana. Jhana is an altered state of consciousness. There are too many teachers out there who try to make jhana seem so difficult that it is "supernatural” and out of the reaches of people who start to meditate. No, that is not the case.

  The Buddha himself talked about how he first experienced his first jhana experience when he was sitting in the backyard waiting for his father. It was only this recall that triggered him to realize that it was this way that was the path to liberation.

  Nothing in the texts ever talk of the jhanas being a state where you cannot hear anything of the outside. Instead, the first jhana talks of "being withdrawn from sensuality and unwholesome mental qualities”. That is all. It means that one has gained a wholesome mental state (having none of the five hindrances) and has become "detached" to the external senses.

  From experience, it is normally in the third jhana where everything starts to become non-dual. Due to upekkha (which is equanimity), you become detached from the senses. It is here when you start to experience a detachment from sensory data from the outside. It is only in fourth jhana, from the abandonment of sukha and dukkha, that one feels completely oblivious to one's surroundings.

  At this point of time, the space between the mind and the physical body has enlarged greatly. It is likely that one has also entered the fifth samadhi, where the mind has transitioned to the manomaya ("body of mind").

  In my opinion, if one is to say that jhana is "hard", in his terms, it is disregarding all the progress that one can make from the first jhana to the third jhana.

  If you read the discourses of the Buddha, you will find that the Buddha gave perfect instructions. His disciple listens, takes the learning, then goes off to practice. In the scriptures, you always see that just by following the instructions, they go through the first, the second, the third and finally the fourth. This is always without fail. His instructions, according to the suttas, are very eloquent (from my personal experience as well).

  I recognize that this is a problem among translations and commentary of suttas as well. They are often done by scholars who have never had attained any fruit from deep experience. As such, they enforce their own understandings on the suttas. When people pick them up and read them, they gain all sorts of erroneous ideas. Language is a very malleable thing. What is said in one word might be interpreted wrongly by another.

  It might be possible that Kenneth Folk had gained the fourth jhana state. However, it might also be possible that he has not gone through all the four jhanas and just ended up at a certain samadhi, which is erroneous. The Buddha had mastered two forms of practice: Samadhi of no-thought and Samadhi of no-thought-nor-perception. These two involved astral travels - Travelling to immaterial domains out of the physical realm. There are thousands of types of samadhi and many ways people can get wrong.

  The Buddha then said that the way to liberation is in the form realm - By mastering the rupa-jhanas (form jhanas). It is only when one has mastery in the rupa-jhanas can one be liberated when going into the arupa-samadhis (immaterial samadhi).

  I hope this helps.

        - **refugeinbuddha** · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · 1 point · 2 years ago

          By the first jhana, it is the case where the hindrances have already weakened. In other words, if one has a weak attention due to torpor, this is not first jhana. In first jhana, it is not an unwholesome mind state. Each time the mind becomes unwholesome, one drops out of jhana. For example, if someone starts to get delusional from the various hallucinations experienced in the mind, they can become fearful or over-excited. This might cause them to lapse in attention and fall out of jhana.

          It is quite distinct from normal consciousness, is it not?

          In normal consciousness, let us say, an unwholesome state, the untrained person has dozens of thoughts floating through his or her mind. In first jhana, there is one-pointedness, applied and sustained, which gives rise to joy and blissfulness.

          When you focus on a meditation object, that is still not one-pointedness. It is only when it is applied and sustained - That is when one-pointedness arises. It is the right degree of attention - not tensing up muscles and yet "placing attention” on the object.

          Of course, there are 'normal’ states that might come closer. For example, when an athlete is focused and in the "flow", you can say that this person is having the trait of one-pointedness. However, because of the lack of physical disengagement from the senses, you cannot call this first jhana. It is similar, but not so.

          It is simply not the same as an unwholesome state. The mind only has two states... An unwholesome state, or a wholesome state.

  1) Yes somewhat. More energy and attention put into the jhana means more energy available for the investigation. It's not that hard jhana has a magic that'll clear things up better, as far as I know, just that it involves more of you so that means more happens to you.

Mostly the habit around here is to say that those "aren't jhana" rather than that jhana is subtle sometimes, which is fine I guess but leaves the question of what to name those subtle jhana-esque things, so w/e.

  3) I wouldn't say "merely"” soft jhana, in my experience it's more difficult to maintain a soft than a hard jhana. Unless you're just too freaked out by it, hard jhana is easy to stay stuck in, because it's simple. All that's there is the jhana, so you just do that, you maintain the thing there is. Soft jhana you're feeding the fire of the jhana and also diverting attention to other things and it dies if you don't get back to it often enough to feed it, it's juggling. If you're in a soft jhana you can get to a hard jhana by putting down the other things so you're no longer juggling, which isn't hard at all intrinsically it's much easier so if it's difficult it's usually because you're avoiding the intensity of giving too much of yourself to the jhana.
