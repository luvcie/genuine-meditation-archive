# Sam Harris: Waking Up and the Nature of Siddhis

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **Sherlockian_Holmes** · *(OCR)* · *אֲהָבִים Ahaviyahu* · `Question` · 5 points · 2 years ago

I am curious, having listened through the beginning of the audiobook, why it appears that Harriss having gone through such extensive meditative retreats never experienced any phenomena that would show him that there might be truth to the fact of chakras, energetic centers, astral body, and so forth.

As you can see from this excerpt in the books here:

> Despite the painful results of my first few days alone in the mountains of Colorado, I later studied with a wide range of monks, lamas, yogis, and other contemplatives, some of whom had lived for decades in seclusion doing nothing but meditating. In the process, I spent two years on silent retreat myself (in increments of one week to three months), practicing various techniques of meditation for twelve to eighteen hours a day. Koesder reports that the aspiring yogi is traditionally encouraged to lengthen his tongue— even going so far as to cut the frenu lum (the membrane that anchors the tongue to the floor of the mouth) and stretch the soft palate. What is the purpose of these modifications? They enable our hero to insert his tongue into his nasopharynx, thereby blocking the flow of air through the nostrils. His anatomy thus improved, a yogi can then imbibe subtle . liquors believed to emanate directly from his brain. These substances— imagined, by recourse to further subtleties, to be connected to the retention of semen— are said to confer not only spiritual wisdom but immortality. This technique of drinking mucus is known as khechari mudra, and it is thought to be one of the crowning achievements of yoga. I'm more than happy to score a point for Koesder here. Needless to say, no defense of such practices will be found in this book. Within most schools of Buddhism, for instance, a buddha—whether the historical Buddha, Siddhartha Gautama, or any other person who attainsthe state of “full enlightenment”—is generally described as “omniscient.” Just what this means is open to a fair bit of caviling. But however narrowly defined, the claim is absurd. If the historical Buddha were “omniscient,” he would have been, at minimum, a better mathematician, physicist, biologist, and Jeopardy contes tant than any person who has ever lived. Is it reasonable to expect that an ascetic in the fifth century BC, by virtue of his meditative insights, spontaneously became an unprecedented genius in every field of human inquiry, including those that did not exist at the time in which he lived? Would Siddhartha Gautama have awed Kurt Godel, Alan Turing, John von Neumann, and Claude Shannon with his command of mathematical logic and information theory? Of course not. To think otherwise is pure, religious piety. Any extension of the notion of "omniscience” to procedural knowledge—that is, to knowing how to do something— would render the Buddha capable of painting the Sistine Chapel in the morning and demolishing Roger Federer at Centre Court in the afternoon. Is there any reason to believe that Siddhartha Gautama, or any other celebrated contemplative, possessed such abilities by virtue of his spiritual practice? None whatsoever. Nevertheless, many Buddhists believe that buddhas can do all these things and more. Again, this is religious dogmatism, not a rational approach to spiritual life. I make no claims in support of magic or miracles in this book. However, I can say that the true goal of meditation is more profound than most people realize—and it does, in fact, encompass many of the experiences that traditional mystics claim for them selves. It is quite possible to lose one's sense of being a separate self and to experience a kind of boundless, open awareness— to feel, in other words, at one with the cosmos. This says a lot about the possibilities of human consciousness, but it says nothing about the universe at large. And it sheds no light at all on the relationship between mind and matter. The fact that it is possible to love one s neighbor as oneself should be a great finding for the field of psychology, but it lends absolutely no credence to the claim that Jesus was the son of God, or even that God exists. Nor does it suggest that the “energy” of love somehow pervades the cosmos. These are historical and metaphysical claims that personal experience cannot justify.

To that end, I find it very strange that a person that has spent several years in complete solitude and used anapanasati meditation would not reach at least 3rd or 4th jhana and at least have experienced either out of body travel, etheric sight or at least have opened Harriss up to the idea of kundalini energy and chi/qi being a real tangible thing.

I am not saying that those things are the goals on the path of attainment, not at all, but I find it strange that a person who commited himself to the Path so fully and studied under Yogis and Sages would not at least experience or see things that would make him question his reality-tunnel?

I'd appreciate any thoughts on this.

This is something that is usually done spontaneously in cessation of the breath in deep samadhi. The tongue rolls backwards to the uvula, and a sweet fragrance descends. Cutting the frenulum is one of the extreme ascetic methods, along with practices such as inserting a cloth into the digestive tract to "clean" it. The Buddha has never recommended it.

> Is it reasonable to expect that an ascetic in the fifth century BC, by virtue of his meditative insights, spontaneously became an unprecedented genius in every field of human inquiry, including those that did not exist at the time in which he lived? Would Siddhartha Gautama have awed Kurt Godel, Alan Turing, John von Neumann, and Claude Shannon with his command of mathematical logic and information theory? Of course not. He does not understand the nature of the intuitive stream, hence his logic is inaccurate. Having enlightenment does not mean that one becomes a genius in all matters. It is simply that when one is enlightened within constriction of the physical body, one can access the intuitive stream and channel it, only if there is a suitable vehicle for it. Hence, if one is not learnt in a specific field of physics, one cannot simply channel his insights into that vehicle. You must remember that knowledge, as defined in the world, is memory - “trapped” insights. When a person is enlightened, direct knowledge comes, but there must be a vehicle for it.

It is however, not strange that many enlightened masters, are highly skilled and trained in many fields and are polymaths. For example, Gautama Siddharta was educated to be a king, and became a master of mathematics, science, literature, martial arts, etc of his time in the Sakyan clan. Another example, is one of my late teachers, Nan Huai Jin, who was a master of eastern history, chinese literature, philosophy, eastern medicine, martial arts, etc. While it increases one's ability to master a field, as a result of merit in previous lifetimes, enlightenment does not mean that one suddenly becomes knowledgeable in every field. Wisdom is not knowledge, but it certainly helps in having deeper level insights.

I do agree with him, that people have very inaccurate perceptions of enlightened beings. They glorify the being, not seeing that this person has simply realized the immensity of being human.

If he says that, he should perhaps take a look at cosmology, either in the Vedic sciences or Buddhist archives. The ancients, without instruments, simply by the power of the mind, described the microcosms (micro-organisms, subatomic particles, etc) and the macrocosms (galaxies, universes, elliptical orbits, etc). They even knew the exact time it took for a galaxy to orbit, the exact speed of light, etc.

Without the filters of the 8 consciousnesses, direct wisdom can be had. By training certain faculties or siddhis, simply by touching the earth of a mountain, one can remember all of its stored memory right back to the start of the Earth.

> To that end, I find it very strange that a person that has spent several years in complete solitude and used anapanasati meditation would not reach at least 3rd or 4th jhana and at least have experienced either out of body travel, etheric sight or at least have opened Harriss up to the idea of kundalini energy and chi/qi being a real tangible thing.

There are many possibilities. When one does not admit he does not know, there is no humility and contentment, and no drive to seek the dharma. With doubt, one of the five hindrances, one cannot proceed. I have also met many erroneous practitioners who treat meditation as a mental game of some sort. They have no attainments, despite years of practice. They do not understand that meditation is beyond mental, it is a divine experience.

When Ananda was next to the Buddha, he served as his attendant. Many people asked: "Why does Ananda, cousin of the Buddha, having been by your side for so many years, not attained enlightenment?” The Buddha replied that a spoon cannot taste the soup. It was only after the Buddha's death that Ananda gained enlightenment.

When one is filled to the brim like a full cup, nothing else can be added to it. Humility means to surrender internally and completely. To hold one's thoughts as pride, that is the work of ego.

I however, do support that there should not be Buddhists, Christians, Sufists, etc - And that any kind of spirituality that forms an ideology, is no longer individual. It becomes a problem where an individual is not willing to investigate for himself - One tries to find someone to follow, to latch onto, thinking that one can ride on an enlightened being to reach enlightenment. That is not true at all, for only the Kingdom can be found within.
