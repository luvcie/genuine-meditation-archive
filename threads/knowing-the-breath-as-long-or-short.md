# Knowing the Breath as Long or Short

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **refugeinbuddha** · *(OCR)* · *परमानन्द Paramananda* · `Advice` · 3 points · 2 months ago

I received a question by a dear friend and thought some might benefit from posting the answer here.

He asked: "What exactly does it mean by knowing the breath as long?"

Now this is something many people are getting wrong - scholars, monks and even entire monasteries. To understand this requires direct experience as well as a keen understanding of the process.

As mentioned before, many people take "ana" and "pana" as simple exhalation and inhalation. A modern understanding that is close is the Yoga's interpretation of the 5 Vayus - with special emphasis on the Prana-vayu and Apana-vayu.

In the Yoga context, Prana-vayu is an upward force while the Apana-vayu is a downward-force. These are just a few examples of how the energy they call prana moves in the body. A similar system also exists in Taoism with the Five Elements.

Now understand this, breath-meditation was not foreign to the Buddha's age. In that age, there were many yogi and holy-men, and there were many who practiced breath exercises. The Buddha himself did a breath-retention practice as evidenced in the Mahasaccaka Sutta.

Therefore, there is a lot of background knowledge presumed which has been lost when scholars take the suttas by themselves without the culture that it was originally in.

What does it mean by knowing the breath as long? The truth is that the suttas translated it as knowing "ana" as long, knowing "apana" as long. A keen understanding of this must be made, but for the sake of simplicity we will use modern words here.

When the "breath" is said to be long, there are three things that happen:

e There will be a long pause before there is a tiny exhalation.

e There will be a long pause before there is a tiny inhalation.

e There will be a long pause in between the exhalations and inhalations.

Nowadays, we have the complete opposite - where people drag out their inhalations or exhalations to extremely long periods such as in certain Pranayama exercises. This is problematic because it increases the strength of delusions. There must be absolute stillness - in the I-ching, the hexagram for Stillness is associated with the message of Growth.

While there are certain physiological effects from deep and prolonged breathing, such as calming one's body down and heart-rate, what this does in a meditative practice is prolong the "false-thoughts” in the mind.

In the mind-science of Buddhism, we know that the movement of breath is highly associated with the movement of thoughts. When thoughts are suddenly cut off, there will not be a breath.

For example, when someone goes into very deep dream-less sleep, their breathing can stop for a few minutes. Similarly, when yogis stop their thoughts, their breathing can stop for miraculously long periods.

Of course, when a meditator's gong-fu achievements have reached this state, then it is possible. For some people, it can take just half a day.

Remember, knowing the breath as long does not mean the external-breath!

In fact, after the external-breath seems to stop, there will be the internal-breath (wind element) which will start to move within. The true Xi is when even this internal breath stops.

The real "long breath"” we are talking about in Buddhism is apana-vayu which drags the prana from the head down to the soles, with every single pore breathing. If the breath reaches the soles, the Taoists call this the attainment of a sage. A common person normally has the breath stop at the throat or lungs. A mystic might have it in the stomach.

The longer the breath, the higher the gong-fu attainment. If the external-breath at the nose can stop for an entire hour and above, the mystic will not drown in water and cannot burn easily in fire - this is because at this point the body glows and radiates with light and chi - an arm's length around the meditator will be impervious to flame.

Remember, it is not about holding breath. There is a solution if one finds that they are being too obsessive in controlling the breath or having difficulties with a chaotic mind. If you find that your mind is going wild, then what you should do is to focus on the out-going breath - and along with it, expel thoughts, delusions, sicknesses, discomfort, etc - and forget the body and mind. If you do this correctly on the natural exhale, it will be very beneficial.

Strive well friends.

May you attain enlightenment in this lifetime.

  One example of this is by the Thai Forest master Phra Ajaan Lee who clearly demonstrates his knowledge of internal-breath as quoted below:

  > Then let the breath from the base of the skull spread down over both shoulders, past your elbows and wrists, to the tips of your fingers, and out into the air. Let the breath at the base of the throat spread down the central nerve at the front of the body, past the lungs and liver, all the way down to the bladder and colon. Inhale the breath right at the middle of the chest and let it go all the way down to your intestines. Let all these breath sensations spread so that they connect and flow together, and you'll feel a greatly improved sense of well-being. Unite the breath sensations throughout the body, letting them flow together comfortably, keeping your awareness as broad as possible. Once you're fully aware of the aspects of the breath you already know in your body, you'll come to know all sorts of other aspects as well. The breath, by its nature, has many facets: breath sensations flowing in the nerves, those flowing around and about the nerves, those spreading from the nerves to every pore. Beneficial breath sensations and harmful ones are mixed together by their very nature.

  Now the only problem with this method is that people will misunderstand his instructions when he says to "adjust” the breathing.

  The right understanding is that he is not advocating a conscious, cognitive change based on what the meditator thinks is right - instead, it is letting go of rigid control so that the body is able to adapt properly and go into a long or short breath according to what it wants.

  So the first point to note about his instructions is that it is not consciously controlling the breath, but understanding what the body wants, and letting it do it. For example, if you are subtly controlling the breath and making it tense and short, then if you let go - the body will adapt accordingly and take in a deep breath if it wants to. Your conscious control is kept out of the way.

  Secondly, Ajaan Lee promotes focal points for the awareness to rest in. This is to help beginners. The higher method is to not be located in any particular position - aka omnipresent.

  Lastly, he explains what he means by 'spreading’ the breath:

  > Spread your awareness — your sense of conscious feeling — throughout the entire body.

  This means one simply has to know - not consciously and forcefully spread feelings around the body.

  Hopefully this tiny part can increase the understanding of the internal-breath.
