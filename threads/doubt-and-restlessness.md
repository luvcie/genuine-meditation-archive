# Doubt and Restlessness

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **He_of_the_Hairy_Arms** · *(OCR)* · `Question` · 3 points · 1 year ago

Hello, sangha,

I am realizing that the main hindrances I've faced for the past year or two are doubt and restlessness. The doubt has caused the restlessness, and the restlessness has also caused the doubt. A lot of both have been caused by uncertainty over how to understand the Satipathana sutta. I have asked a lot of questions and done a lot of research, but I still have questions that remain.

The part of the sutta concerning breath meditation is as follows:

‘And how does a monk dwell contemplating the body in the body? Here a monk, gone to the forest, to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, sits down; having folded his legs crosswise, straightened his body, and established mindfulness in front of him, mindful he breathes in, and mindful he breathes out. Breathing in long, he understands: "I breathe in long”; or breathing out long, he understands: "I breathe out long.” Breathing in short, he understands: "I breathe in short”; or breathing out short, he understands: "I breathe out short.” He trains thus: I will breathe out experiencing the whole body. He trains thus: “I will breathe out experiencing the whole body.” He trains thus: “I will breathe in tranquilizing the bodily formation”; he trains thus: I will breathe out tranquilizing the bodily formation.” Just as a skilled lathe worker or his apprentice, when making a long turn, understands: "I make a long turn”; or, when making a short turn, understands: "I make a short turn”; so too, breathing in long, a monk understands: “I breathe in long”; or, when breathing out long, understands: "I breathe out long."..he trains thus: "I will breathe out tranquilizing the bodily formation.”****

There appears to be much debate over the intended meaning of the phrase, “established mindfulness in front of him.” Some think it means “in front of him,” meaning “establishing it as the goal or priority,” and others think it means “around the nose.”

This influences how to read the following line, which, despite so much questioning and receiving in-depth answers, I still question. I suppose I simply don‘t know why the Buddha gave the instruction like this. If he does not mean “establish mindfulness around the nose,” then understanding the lengths of the breaths seems to be a very diffuse sort of attention, with some thinking and conceptualizing mixed in with noting sensations. The emphasis on one-pointedness and simplicity feels lost. On the other hand, if the Buddha means to focus on sensations around the nose, consistently, then it seems that telling students to “"know the lengths” is unnecessary, misleading, and confusing. Why would the Buddha give this instruction when it is likely to be misinterpreted and lead to multi-pointedness and analytical thought? Either way, there seem to be problems.

This in turn influences how to read the following lines. Because of some word ambiguity, “experiencing the whole body” is debated as meaning either “experiencing the whole length of the breath,” or, quite simply “experiencing the whole body” in a holistic way. If it should be taken the first way, then it seems to be a further clarification, maybe undoing the ambiguity of “know the lengths” when attending to sensations around the nose. Perhaps, in this reading, “experience the whole body” is the final condition in the instruction to “know the whole lengths of the breath, without missing a moment.” Still, why not say it that way initially? On the other hand, if it means simply to know the whole body, then there seems to be the problem of multi-pointedness again. How does one balance the attention between the whole body and one’s breathing? How does one ensure they aren't just switching back and forth in short succession? Maybe more importantly, if this is not part of the same basic instruction on breath meditation, when and how does one switch from knowing the lengths to knowing the whole body?

I have also watched Master Nan’s video on how the Buddha originally taught anapana, but still noticed some logical paradoxes there. For example, if one is supposed to be aware of the length of time that the breath has ceased, then how is one’s mind completely still during that cessation? If one’s mind ceases, one doesn’t note how long it ceases— because it has ceased. So does one retrospectively decide how long the mind had ceased, when one is supposed to be attending to the next breath? And if noting the cessations happens automatically as part of simply noting the breaths from beginning to end, then why give it as an instruction at all?

I feel the need to apologize for dragging any readers through my maze of confusion, but these things are genuine road blocks for me. And there are so many possibilities for the meanings of the sutta that it’s difficult to try all of them for long enough to see what works and what doesn‘t. And, I have achieved jhana (when I was in my “honeymoon phase” of beginner’s mind) with and without emphasizing the sensations around my nose. So I know that both can work, but am not sure which way works better and more reliably.

I will say this: I have begun reading The Mind Illuminated, by Culadasa, who takes the approach of noting the sensations around the nose and returning the breath to those sensations. I also have read Right Concentration, by Leigh Brasington, who emphasizes the same. My intuition tells me both are genuinely accomplished in the jhanas, and since going back to the basics, I've approached the “qi” stage more consistently. Still, I want the full confidence of knowing I am doing things the way the Buddha himself instructed.

Any comments would be appreciated, and thank you for bearing with me.

- **refugeinbuddha** · *परमानन्द Paramananda*

  It is not noticing the nose, nor the breath at the nose, but simply knowing the breath. In other words, the expansion and contraction, the movement of "wind" in and out of the body.

  Let us say you and I sit around a dining table. At the point that we sit there, we know that we are sitting at the table with food on it. However, past that point of knowing, the mental discrimination rises and one starts to have all sorts of thoughts. It is the same as meditation. At the point of sitting, one knows. That is the prime point of entry into absorption. However, for most minds, the thought "I am now meditating" occurs, along with various other delusions that play along in the background.

  Do not get this wrong, the knowing nature simply has the function of knowing, but it in itself is empty.

  The Buddha phrased it in perspective of the meditator. Why? Because body and mind are not part of the true Buddha-nature or citta. That is why the usage indicates a certain detached watching. But if you deeply meditate, you will find that the watcher and the watched arise together as intertwined notions, movements of mind.

  In the end, one enters jhana because of the mind state. This is especially so when one cuts out of the outflows of the five senses. Lust for form, fragrance, flavors, sounds, and touch is what restrains a person from achieving the pliant and bright mental state as a prerequisite.
