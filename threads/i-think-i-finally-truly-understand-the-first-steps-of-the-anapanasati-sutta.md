# I think I finally, truly understand the first steps of the Anapanasati Sutta

**r/genuinemeditation** · by **He_of_the_Hairy_Arms** · *(OCR)* · `Discussion` · 4 points · 9 months ago

Hello, sangha,

I've recently had what I think is an epiphany about meditation, and wanted to run it by the group.

For a long time I was unsure how to reconcile the teachings of the Buddha on anapanasati with my own limited experiences with the first jhana, and with other meditation teachers and traditions. For example, I wondered, "Why did the Buddha instruct us to understand that the breath is long or short, when so many jhana teachers instruct students to attend to tactile sensations around the nose? And if attending to tactile sensations is what the Buddha really meant, then why would the teaching be worded in such an un-intuitive way?"

This undermined my faith and unsettled my meditation practice. Always in the back of my mind, when I noted tactile sensations, there was the question of whether I should instead know "long" or "short," and when I was knowing "long" or "short," there was always the question of whether I should switch to noting tactile sensations.

However, after a long meditation session last night, followed by a long session of journaling and contemplating, I think I've found an answer. Sitting on my cushion journaling, I thought back to the time that I was actually achieving first jhana regularly. What I remembered was that my attention on the breath was similar to one's attention on a swinging pendulum, or one of those desktop wave machines that has dyed water that sloshes back and forth. There was some awareness, at all times, of attending to just one part of a cycle--back and forth, back and forth. Then I tried to apply the analogy of the pendulum or wave machine more precisely to the breath. I asked myself what it was, exactly, that the observer of the pendulum does when he follows it with his eyes. The observer is not so locked into the bottom of the pendulum that all he sees is a circle, with no sense of the pendulum's motion. Although the bottom of the pendulum is useful in providing the eye with something to follow, there is also the thought, even if subtle, of "now it goes this way,” and "now it goes that way." This, I decided, was analogous to the meditator knowing, "now the breath goes in," or "now the breath goes out." Paramananda, in one of his responses to me, did say that tactile sensations provide the feedback necessary for the intuitive knowing of the breath's lengths, but I didn't fully understand that until (I think) just recently.

Having decided that knowing whether the breath is going in or out is the basic task, I then ventured to understand "if the breath is long/short, he knows it is long/short.” In the context of the basic observation being "in" or "out,"” the instruction, "if the breath is long/short, he knows it is long/short,"” means something like, "However long your breath lasts--whether a long, short, or medium length of time--you understand ‘air going in' or 'air going out' for that whole time." In other words, the importance is not the length, but that the attention to “in" or "out"” exactly matches the length of time the phenomenon is happening, without any labeling and moving on prematurely, or dwelling after the breath is over. Presence with the breath exactly as it occurs is what is meant.

This may seem like a very obvious and basic thing to spend so much time trying to understand. But I really was confounded trying to understand how the instructions in the Suttas and that of contemporary teachers and even the commentaries could possibly all be the same teaching. I know that sometimes they are not, but for example in the Burmese or Thai traditions, extreme emphasis is placed on tactile sensation, which is not the Suttas' exact teachings, yet I think it implausible that none of them are achieving jhanas. So this little revelation of mine, I think, establishes the relationship between the "lengths of the breaths," the "knowing sense," and the tactile sensations that contribute to but are just implied in that knowing sense.

Any comments or corrections are welcome.

EDIT: I have also, in my own typical and mildly obsessive fashion, tried to research what lathe-working looked like in the time of the Buddha. I'm still unsure, but I think the analogy is mostly meant to point out the lathe-worker's constancy of attention to every "turn” he makes. Also implied in the analogy is attention to the point of contact, but again, this could be just a necessary component of his knowing what he does for the whole time he does it.

  That is wonderful insight, good progress. You are doing great, keep up the good work. Now to move one step ahead, one should also realize that this "awareness" does not stop at all.

  The external breath has four components:

  1. Inhalation

  2. Gap between inhale and exhale

  3. Exhalation

  4. Gap after exhale

  What you will realize is that the knowing does stop during the gaps. It feels like there is nothing much to know in those gaps. This has the function of Resting. Rest and movement indicate growth and depletion respectively.

  This is when you may realize that every action, speech or thought is a form of movement and hence a depletion. Whenever there is rest, there is a form of growth, of recharging, of rejuvenation.

  Therefore, this means that as you are able to calm your body and mind down by relaxing, releasing hindrances, and returning gently to the breath, the gaps will start to become longer and the movements will start to be shorter. This is approaching the function of resting.

  As such, the mind has to be more expansive, such that is is aware of anything that arises, not constricted and tight. A lightness of ease, as if one is on vacation and relaxing. From this ease comes a sense of relief and expansive joy. This light joy will become the glue that makes your mind effortlessly watch the breath.

                          > I have encountered teachings that say to switch to a pleasant sensation when the breath is faint.

                          In an expansive, tranquil mind-state, it is just like a still pond - when the slightest movement arises, it can be detected easily. This ease of recognizing things is what is important. Hence, it is important to be clear on this. It is not about rejecting phenomena when they arise.

                          When the external breath fades away and spontaneously ceases, the internal breath awakens from the perineum area, and this is called the kundalini or 'yang chi’ - And this is felt as the tactile jhana-nimitta (blissfulness) and joyous feelings.

                          At this point there is no more external breath, so one continues to hold that expansive knowing-nature, and simply knows the ‘inner-breath’. You can see more of these instructions in the Anapanasati series - long/short, whole body, etc.

                          The key is to continue calming down the mind and body agitations - such that it becomes as still and at ease as possible. When the stillness reaches an extreme, the blissfulness arises in full-force, and one can observe as it makes its way through the blockages of the body, manifesting as itching, heat, coldness, numbness, and all kinds of sensations.
